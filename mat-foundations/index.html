In Basildon, raft and mat foundation design starts with that profile: a near-surface crust of firm brown clay over grey-blue clay of medium to high plasticity, often softened where perched water sits above the clay matrix. Getting the bearing stratum right means the difference between a slab that settles uniformly and one that tilts within the first wet winter. We run the numbers through BS EN 1997-1:2004 Design Approach 1, factoring in undrained shear strength from triaxial tests and consolidation parameters from oedometer cells. Methodology applied in Basildon

A three-storey apartment block off Nethermayne presented a textbook Basildon problem: stiff clay at 1.2 metres, softening to firm by 2.8 metres, with a thin lens of silty sand at the transition that held water after heavy rain. The structural engineer wanted a 250 mm ground-bearing slab with perimeter thickening, but the bearing capacity at formation level was marginal — 95 kPa undrained, against a column load demand of 130 kPa under dead plus imposed. Rather than deepen the excavation into the wet sand lens, we designed a compensated raft 400 mm thick with a 600 mm deep edge beam, spreading the load to keep contact pressure below 80 kPa. The atterberg-limits profile confirmed the clay was high-plasticity CH with a liquidity index near 0.4, so we modelled long-term settlement using one-dimensional consolidation theory with mv values from incremental loading tests. Predicted total settlement came in at 22 mm, differential under 8 mm, both within the BS 8004 serviceability limits for framed structures on stiff clay. Reinforcement was detailed with crack-control steel at 150 mm centres in both directions, and the slab was cast on a 150 mm compacted granular blanket to break capillary rise.

Risks and considerations in Basildon

Basildon's post-war expansion turned farmland into New Town residential estates at speed, and not every plot got the ground investigation it deserved. Much of the town centre and the industrial estates along the A132 sit on made ground of variable thickness: brick rubble, ash, reworked clay, and occasional pockets of organic silt that decompose over decades. Under a raft foundation, that heterogeneity translates into differential settlement that cracks partition walls and jams doors — problems we see in 1960s-era low-rise blocks where thin ground-bearing slabs were poured directly onto unengineered fill. The London Clay beneath adds a second risk: its shrink-swell potential is moderate to high, and trees planted too close to the slab edge extract moisture unevenly, inducing seasonal heave cycles. We mitigate this with deeper edge beams penetrating below the active zone, root barriers where retaining mature trees, and — on poor ground — substituting the raft with a stone-columns grid that densifies the fill and provides a uniform modulus of subgrade reaction. A desk study of historical maps is mandatory before any raft design in Basildon; the route of the old Plotlands drainage ditches still runs under some residential streets.

When is a raft foundation preferable to strip footings on London Clay?

A raft becomes the better choice when the allowable bearing pressure is below 100 kPa, when differential settlement across strip footings would exceed 1/500, or when the site has a history of fill or desiccation cracking. The raft bridges soft spots and spreads column loads so contact pressure stays low enough for the stiff clay to perform reliably through wet-dry cycles.

How do trees near the site affect raft foundation design in Basildon?

Trees draw moisture from the clay, causing shrinkage that can extend to depths of 2.5 metres or more. We calculate the required edge beam depth using NHBC Chapter 4.2 guidance based on tree species, mature height, and distance from the slab. In high-influence zones, we specify deeper perimeter beams, compressible void formers, or root barriers to isolate the raft from seasonal volume changes.
