A recent foundation investigation near the A127 corridor in Basildon encountered a classic problem: soft alluvial clays overlying a deeply weathered chalk surface, with a suspected buried scour hollow that conventional boreholes kept missing between stations. The solution was a targeted electrical resistivity line that imaged the lateral transition from low-resistivity drift into the high-resistivity chalk in a continuous profile, revealing a five-metre depression that explained the erratic SPT refusals encountered earlier. In this part of south Essex, where the London Clay thins and the Lambeth Group sands sit unconformably over Upper Chalk, vertical electrical sounding provides a cost-effective way to map the bedrock interface without drilling every twenty metres. Basildon’s development over former marshland and reclaimed plots means buried channels, variable saturation, and saline intrusion near the Crouch estuary can all affect resistivity signatures: interpreting them requires local experience with the Thames Terrace sequence. We often pair resistivity lines with MASW when shear-wave velocity is also needed for seismic site class, or run a CPT programme to calibrate the geoelectrical boundaries against cone resistance and pore-pressure data.

In Basildon, the electrical step from low-resistivity alluvium into high-resistivity chalk is one of the cleanest geoelectrical markers in the Thames Basin — but only when calibrated against a physical log.

Methodology applied in Basildon

The field setup we deploy across Basildon’s mixed-use sites uses a multi-electrode resistivity meter with 48 to 72 steel electrodes spaced between two and five metres, depending on the target depth. For a typical VES sounding, we expand the current-electrode separation in logarithmic steps out to AB/2 of 100 metres or more, which gives penetration well into the chalk. The instrument injects a controlled low-frequency alternating current and measures the resulting potential difference; apparent resistivity is calculated via the Schlumberger or Wenner array geometry and inverted with solid 2D smoothness-constrained least-squares routines. On a recent job near the Basildon Enterprise Corridor, the survey had to navigate buried services, a live haul road, and a waterlogged balancing pond: the crew used a rolling electrode cable spread with real-time GPS positioning, which allowed the line to bend around obstacles without sacrificing data quality. The processed resistivity tomogram showed a clear three-layer model: high-resistivity made ground over low-resistivity alluvial clay, transitioning into chalk with resistivity above 120 ohm-m. Because the London Clay is absent in much of Basildon, the geoelectrical contrast between drift and chalk is sharper than in north London, which improves resolution but also demands careful calibration with a test pit or window sampler to confirm the top-of-chalk pick.

Risks and considerations in Basildon

What catches out inexperienced surveys in Basildon is the saltwater wedge in the Crouch and Thames estuaries: even a few kilometres inland, pockets of brackish groundwater can drop the bulk resistivity of the superficial deposits below 5 ohm-m, mimicking a clay-rich layer and suppressing the true depth to chalk. The same effect occurs around old landfill cells and infilled gravel pits, where leachate plumes create low-resistivity anomalies that can be misread as geological boundaries. Another local pitfall is the presence of flint bands within the Upper Chalk: these resistive stringers can produce a false double-layer signature that looks like a separate bedrock unit if the inversion is not constrained with borehole control. The technical team cross-references every resistivity section with at least one logged borehole, using the physical stratigraphy to anchor the geoelectrical model and avoid over-interpretation of noise. In areas where the Lambeth Group sands are water-bearing, the resistivity contrast between wet sand and dry chalk can be subtle, so we supplement the survey with grain-size analysis on recovered samples to confirm the lithological boundary.

2D Electrical Resistivity Tomography

Common questions

How much does an electrical resistivity survey cost in Basildon?

For a typical Basildon site with a single 2D resistivity line of 100–150 metres and one VES sounding, the fee ranges from £550 to £850, depending on access conditions, electrode count, and the need for traffic management or weekend working.

What depth can a VES survey reach in the Basildon area?

With a maximum AB/2 current-electrode separation of 100–150 metres, effective investigation depth reaches about 30–50 metres below ground level in Basildon’s drift-and-chalk sequence, sufficient to penetrate through alluvium and into competent chalk.

Can resistivity tell the difference between wet sand and clay?

Partially. Both saturated sand and clay can show low resistivity, so the method alone cannot always distinguish them. The team cross-references resistivity profiles with probe logs or trial pit descriptions to resolve the lithology, particularly where the Lambeth Group sands sit beneath Basildon’s alluvial cover.

How long does a resistivity line take on site?

A standard 48-electrode spread with a roll-along sequence on a Basildon brownfield plot typically takes four to six hours of field time, including setup, cable checks, data acquisition, and demobilisation. Processing and interpretation add another one to two days in the office.
